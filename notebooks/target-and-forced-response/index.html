The target and the forced response

Every member follows the same forcing path by year. Before a model can read ENSO, that shared path has to come out, and “wet” needs one threshold.

Overview¶

  1. Name the test members before anything is fit.

  2. Remove the forced response and check it with a test that can fail.

  3. Define “wet” against one pooled threshold; read the base rate per half.

  4. Read the target against the input.

Shared code lives in lensml.py; show() prints a function’s source.

Name the test members first¶

TEST_FRACTION of each half’s members are drawn from a RandomState seeded with RNG_SEED, so re-running redraws the same members. Nothing is fit on them; chapter 6 scores them. They are not a blind test set (see the note).

Why these test members are not a blind test set

During development these same members were scored with the Niño-sign rule, with logistic and boosting at the base-rate threshold, and, in the companion course, with a small neural network (chapter 6 gives the details), so a result on them is provisional; a fresh test would need data no choice was made on. Chapter 7 uses the same members’ SSP3-7.0 winters.

Remove the forced response¶

The raw SON Niño 3.4 box mean and the winter precipitation both carry a forced trend (the corr(…, year) lines below print it). A degree-FORCED_DEG polynomial in year, fit to the training-member ensemble mean, is subtracted from every member, leaving its own departure: the ENSO state, not the decade. Otherwise the models would learn time-of-century as ENSO skill.

Then the target: wet = 1 when detrended precipitation sits above the WET_QUANTILE of the pooled training members, one threshold for every member. One curve is fit to both forcing halves together; the per-half base-rate check below supports pooling them, though it cannot see every difference a forcing half could make (chapter 6, limit 6).

Why one threshold, fit on training members only

A per-member quantile would fix each member’s base rate by construction, and the per-half check below could never fail. Curve and threshold are fit on training members and applied to everyone else, as a StandardScaler inside a Pipeline is (scikit-learn’s Common pitfalls, §12.1–12.2), so every validation fold in chapters 3–5 refits them (chapter 3’s arm B skips the refit on purpose, to measure the leak).

The check that can fail¶

A forced path the curve missed survives as decades whose mean anomaly sits away from zero. The removal passes only if every decade’s mean lies within ±DECADE_TOL standard errors; the forced-response removal line gives the verdict and the chance of a false alarm.

Why not test the correlation with year

The correlation of the anomaly with year is zero by construction for any FORCED_DEG >= 1, so it could never fail.

DECADE_TOL = 3.0
d = table[is_train]
decade = (d["winter"] // 10) * 10
for anom, raw in ANOMALIES.items():
    r = d[raw].corr(d["winter"])
    print(
        f"corr({raw:20s}, year) = {r:+.3f}   "
        "(the forced trend in the raw series, training members)"
    )
removal_ok, n_tests = True, 0
for anom in ANOMALIES:
    g = d.groupby(decade)[anom]
    z = g.mean() / (d[anom].std() / np.sqrt(g.size()))
    worst = z.abs().idxmax()
    ok = bool((z.abs() <= DECADE_TOL).all())
    removal_ok &= ok
    n_tests += len(z)
    print(
        f"{anom:12s} decade-mean anomaly in standard errors: worst {z[worst]:+.2f} "
        f"(the {worst}s) · tolerance ±{DECADE_TOL:.0f} → {'PASS' if ok else 'FAIL'}"
    )
false_alarm = 1 - (1 - 2 * norm.sf(DECADE_TOL)) ** n_tests
verdict = (
    "PASS"
    if removal_ok
    else "FAIL — a forced path survived; change FORCED_DEG"
)
print(
    f"forced-response removal: {verdict}   ({n_tests} decade means; chance a "
    f"correct removal trips the tolerance somewhere ≈ {false_alarm:.0%})"
)

The base rate, per half¶

With WET_QUANTILE = 2/3 the base rate sits near one third, so “always dry” scores about two-thirds accuracy, so this cookbook reports precision and recall (scikit-learn’s Classification metrics guide) against the base rate. Reading the straight-line relation. NWS Boulder’s sign for east of the Divide is positive — El Niño episodes wetter, La Niña drier — and, for La Niña, the opposite (wetter) for the northwest and north-central mountains, the latter partly in the box’s west column. Its El Niño-wet half is stated “particularly during the late winter and spring of moderate to strong El Niños”, of which DJF holds only February; its La Niña-dry half for southwest and eastern Colorado in “the entire cold season of moderate to strong La Niñas”, while it stresses “the fall and spring of La Niña episodes” for one pattern east of the Divide (the northwest jet). So DJF tests the La Niña-dry half in its stated season and holds only February of the El Niño-wet half. Read the per-member sign count line: do the members share a sign? The standard errors line says whether the mean is distinguishable from zero. Stagge et al. set the observed context: over a climatological model, “the ENSO term explained an additional 2% of total variance” in their regional model, a share of variance across gauges, which they say “should be viewed in the context of extreme spatial and elevation precipitation differences”, and their northern dipole model (“North–Wyoming”) is bounded by 41.2° to 45.5° N. By latitude the Front Range, at about 38.5–40.5° N, falls outside the North–Wyoming model and sits mostly between the poles, its northern edge just past 40° N, though their Fig. 1b shades Colorado’s central mountains, partly in the box’s west column, with the northern pole’s sign; and in the northern pole the sign is reversed, with “increased winter precipitation during La Niña events”.

The one input, and the ones not chosen¶

One input keeps the model readable whole. Any other field is fair only as an autumn value, known by the start of the winter (at zero lead for a SON mean); NWS Pueblo’s outlook names PNA and NAO as the patterns with the “highest correlations wet/dry”, but as patterns to watch during the winter, which it calls “extremely difficult to forecast more than a few weeks in advance”; whether an autumn value carries into DJF is untested here.

A new input means a new box mean in derive_member_winters and a rebuild, so it is a costed next change, never a quiet edit to FEATURES.

Summary¶

Test members were named first. The forced response came out with a curve fit on training members, checked by a test that can fail. “Wet” is one pooled threshold, so the per-half base rates are a real check. The straight-line relation is weak; the decile table shows a shape a line misses.
